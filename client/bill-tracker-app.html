How to pick a
bill tracker app.

Most bill trackers look identical on a feature list and feel completely different after a month of real use. These are the seven things that actually decide it — and an honest note on where FiHaven lands on each.

1. Is bank linking required?

This is the biggest fork in the road. Aggregators are near-useless without a connection; manual-first apps work the moment you type something in. Neither is wrong — but decide before you sign up, because it's hard to switch camps later. FiHaven is manual-first; Plaid linking is an optional Pro extra.

2. Do the schedules match real life?

Real bills aren't all monthly. Look for weekly through annual, active date windows for seasonal bills, and a way to say "not this cycle" without deleting the bill. FiHaven covers all three, on the free tier.

3. Can you pay part of something?

Plenty of trackers treat a bill as paid or unpaid and nothing between. If you ever split a payment across two paydays, you need partial payments or your totals will lie to you. FiHaven supports partial payments free.

4. Does it think about debt, not just bills?

A bill is due once. A balance compounds. If you carry card or loan debt, you want payoff strategies — snowball, avalanche, minimums — and visibility into promo-rate windows. FiHaven has the payoff planner in Pro; cards and loans themselves are free.

5. Does it work with no signal?

You will open this app in a basement, on a plane, in a car park. If it's a thin web wrapper it'll show you a spinner. FiHaven keeps a local copy on every platform; offline edits are saved to the device first and sync later.

6. Can you get your data out?

Mint's shutdown taught everyone this one. Check for real export — JSON or CSV — before you enter two years of history. FiHaven exports both, any time, on the free tier.

7. Does anyone else need to see it?

If you split rent or run a household, single-user tools get old fast. Look at whether sharing costs everyone a subscription. FiHaven's Family plan covers a household of three, and members join free.

The quiet eighth: how it's funded

Free apps are usually paid for with ads, partner offers, or data. That's not automatically bad, but it shapes what the app nudges you toward. FiHaven takes no ads and sells no data — only optional subscriptions.

Where FiHaven isn't the answer

FiHaven is a bill, card, and loan dashboard with budgeting around the edges. It is not an investment tracker, not a tax tool, and not a full financial aggregator that pulls every account into one automatically categorised feed. If that's the job, tools like Monarch Money, Copilot, or Quicken Simplifi are built for it. If you want a calm, manual-first view of what's due and what you owe — on every device, offline, without ads — that's the thing FiHaven is actually good at.
